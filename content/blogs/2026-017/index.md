{{< summary title="Abstract" >}}

Understanding how cells regulate gene expression is a longstanding goal of biology. Deep learning has advanced this effort through sequence-to-function models that predict functional genomic measurements from DNA and cell state models that learn patterns of coordinated gene expression. Connecting these approaches could help predict how sequence effects depend on the cellular environment and how regulatory sequence shapes responses to regulator perturbations. Here, we review emerging approaches to joint modeling and examine how biological measurements, model design, and training data shape the regulatory relationships they can learn. We discuss evaluations that test generalization to unmeasured sequences and cellular conditions, alongside interpretation methods that generate mechanistic hypotheses. We then consider how incorporating cellular dynamics could help predict the propagation and persistence of regulatory responses. Finally, we discuss how pilot scaling studies could guide data acquisition and how AI agents could support model development, interpretation, and experimental design.

## Introduction

Understanding how cells regulate gene expression is a longstanding challenge in biology. Transcription depends on interactions between *cis*-regulatory DNA, *trans*-acting factors such as transcription factors (TFs) and cofactors, and chromatin state<sup>[1](#ref-1)</sup>. These interactions allow cells with nearly identical genomes to maintain distinct identities and respond to developmental and environmental signals. A sequence change may alter transcription only when particular regulators are active<sup>[2](#ref-2)</sup>, while changing regulator activity can affect many loci and initiate further regulatory responses<sup>[3](#ref-3)</sup>. A predictive understanding of these relationships would connect genetic variation to its cellular consequences and support the design of interventions that alter gene expression in specific contexts. The possible combinations of sequences, regulator activities, and cellular conditions far exceed what experiments can exhaustively measure, motivating AI models that learn from available data to predict regulatory activity in unmeasured conditions and responses to new interventions.

AI modeling of gene regulation has developed along two largely separate directions, one centered on regulatory DNA sequence and the other on cellular state. Regulatory sequence modeling uses sequence-to-function (seq2func) models to predict molecular measurements, including chromatin accessibility and gene expression, from DNA<sup>[4](#ref-4),[5](#ref-5)</sup>. These models support variant-effect prediction and the design of regulatory sequences with specified activities<sup>[6](#ref-6),[7](#ref-7)</sup>. Model interpretation can also help decipher *cis*-regulatory grammar by examining how the identity, spacing, and combinations of TF-binding sites contribute to regulatory activity<sup>[8](#ref-8)</sup>. Cell state models learn representations from molecular profiles, primarily gene expression, to characterize cellular variation<sup>[9](#ref-9)</sup>. These representations support cell type annotation and integration across datasets, helping identify cell populations and expression programs associated with development or disease. Models trained on perturbation measurements aim to predict responses to genetic or pharmacological interventions<sup>[10](#ref-10)</sup>. Extending these capabilities to new combinations of sequence and cellular state requires predicting how the regulatory environment modifies sequence effects. In sequence-only models, each output is trained to predict a molecular readout in a specified cellular context, while transcriptomics-based cell state models generally leave regulatory DNA implicit.

Emerging approaches have begun to connect sequence and cell state modeling by conditioning sequence predictions on cellular measurements or incorporating sequence information into models of cellular regulation<sup>[11](#ref-11)–[16](#ref-16)</sup>. Joint models could, in principle, predict how a variant’s effect depends on regulator abundance or activity and how the response to a regulator perturbation depends on regulatory sequence. Learning regulatory relationships shared across loci and cellular states could extend these predictions to sequence–context combinations not observed during training. These capabilities would support identifying the cellular contexts and mechanisms through which disease-associated variants act, designing regulatory sequences with specified activities across cellular states, and selecting regulator interventions that alter gene expression in a given genetic background. Grounding these models in cellular dynamics could help predict how regulatory effects propagate over time and whether interventions produce lasting changes in cellular state.

Here, we review emerging approaches that connect regulatory sequence with cellular state to predict context-dependent gene regulation. We examine which cellular measurements provide context, how sequence and context are represented, and how their representations are combined. We then consider how training data support learning regulatory relationships, how evaluation tests their predictive value across sequences and cellular states, and how interpretation generates mechanistic hypotheses for experimental testing. Finally, we discuss how grounding models in cellular dynamics, guiding data acquisition through scaling studies, and using AI agents for model development, interpretation, and experimental design could accelerate their development and expand their biological applications.

## Interplay between regulatory sequence and cellular state

Transcriptional output of a gene depends on its *cis*-regulatory sequence, the *trans*-regulatory environment, and local chromatin features<sup>[1](#ref-1)</sup> (**Figure 1**). Here, **cell state** includes the chromatin environment and the abundance and activity of *trans*-acting regulators.

![**Figure 1. TFs, cofactors, and chromatin regulators interpret sequence features while also shaping the chromatin environment in which they act. Gene expression can in turn alter regulator abundance, connecting regulation at individual loci to changes in cellular state. RNA processing and decay, translation, and protein turnover influence this relationship, while protein localization and post-translational modifications can change regulatory activity without corresponding changes in RNA abundance. Extracellular signals and mechanical or metabolic cues act through these regulatory processes, and secreted proteins can influence the cell and its neighbors. The circled P represents protein post-translational modifications, illustrated by phosphorylation. The chromatin self-loop denotes maintenance of features established by prior regulatory activity<sup>[17](#ref-17)</sup>. Regulatory RNAs can act in *cis* or *trans*, with XIST-mediated silencing illustrating RNA-dependent chromatin regulation on the chromosome from which the RNA is transcribed<sup>[18](#ref-18)</sup>. Bolts mark experimental intervention points. Perturbations are classified relative to the focal gene, with changes to its linked regulatory elements affecting *cis* regulation and changes to the abundance or activity of regulators acting on it affecting its *trans*-regulatory environment.](fig1-sequence-state-interplay.png "width=1000")

The ***trans*-regulatory environment** includes TFs, cofactors, chromatin regulators and regulatory RNAs that act in *trans*. Their regulatory effects depend on their abundance, interactions with molecular partners, biochemical state and subcellular localization<sup>[19](#ref-19),[20](#ref-20)</sup>. For proteins, post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation and changes in localization can alter activity without a corresponding change in RNA abundance<sup>[21](#ref-21),[22](#ref-22)</sup>. Extracellular signals and mechanical or metabolic cues can modify these properties, allowing the *trans*-regulatory environment to change as cells respond to their surroundings<sup>[23](#ref-23)–[25](#ref-25)</sup>.

***Cis*-regulatory elements (CREs)** are DNA sequences that regulate transcription of genes on the same DNA molecule. Their effects depend on the regulatory proteins they recruit and their interactions with other regulatory elements. CREs include promoters, which support transcription initiation, and enhancers, which can modulate transcription from target promoters over short or long genomic distances<sup>[1](#ref-1)</sup>. Within individual elements, TF binding depends on both the recognized motif and its local sequence context. Flanking nucleotides, for example, can influence binding affinity through direct DNA contacts and effects on DNA shape and flexibility<sup>[26](#ref-26),[27](#ref-27)</sup>. Surrounding sequence can also tune binding through weak interactions that locally enrich TFs, as observed for short tandem repeats<sup>[28](#ref-28)</sup>. Binding by one TF can also influence the occupancy of other factors through cooperative or competitive interactions. These interactions can depend on the identities and affinities of the binding sites, their spacing and orientation, and the concentrations of the available TFs<sup>[20](#ref-20),[29](#ref-29),[30](#ref-30)</sup>. The same regulatory sequence can therefore support different binding configurations as regulator availability changes.

The **chromatin state** includes nucleosome organization, chromatin accessibility, histone modifications, DNA methylation, and three-dimensional genome organization<sup>[31](#ref-31)</sup>. These properties influence regulatory-factor binding and communication between regulatory elements, while being shaped by DNA sequence and regulator activity. Nucleosomes can restrict access to binding sites, although some TFs can bind nucleosomal DNA<sup>[32](#ref-32)</sup>. DNA methylation can increase or decrease binding affinity depending on the TF and sequence<sup>[33](#ref-33)</sup>. TFs can in turn recruit histone-modifying enzymes and nucleosome remodelers through their effector domains, helping establish or alter accessibility and transcription<sup>[34](#ref-34),[35](#ref-35)</sup>. At larger genomic scales, three-dimensional organization influences communication between distal enhancers and promoters<sup>[36](#ref-36)</sup>. Sequence-dependent changes in regulator expression can in turn alter the *trans*-regulatory environment at other loci, connecting regulation of individual genes to changes in cellular state<sup>[37](#ref-37)</sup>.

Regulatory measurements reflect the combined effects of sequence, chromatin, and the *trans*-regulatory environment. Comparisons that hold sequence or environment constant help distinguish these contributions. Testing the same sequence features across cellular states reveals whether their contributions change with context. At the *Drosophila shavenbaby* locus, enhancer dissection showed that some TF-binding sites contribute to expression in both embryos and pupae, whereas others have stage-specific contributions<sup>[38](#ref-38)</sup>. Comparing different sequences in a shared environment addresses the contribution of sequence variation. In F1 hybrids, parental alleles share the same *trans*-acting factors, so allelic differences in expression or TF binding support *cis*-regulatory effects. Comparing these differences with those between the parents helps identify contributions from their distinct *trans* environments<sup>[39](#ref-39),[40](#ref-40)</sup>.

Interpreting these comparisons requires specifying which molecular quantity is measured. Nascent RNA assays report ongoing transcription, whereas conventional RNA sequencing measures RNA abundance, which also depends on processing and decay<sup>[41](#ref-41)</sup>. When RNA profiles are used to characterize cellular state, regulator RNA abundance does not necessarily reflect the corresponding protein abundance or activity. Following irradiation, p53 protein levels oscillate through changes in protein stability while *TP53* mRNA remains unchanged<sup>[42](#ref-42)</sup>. Expression of downstream targets may provide indirect evidence of this response, but the regulator’s own RNA abundance does not report the protein dynamics driving it.

Destructive sequencing assays also leave individual-cell transitions unobserved. Combining genetic perturbations with single-cell RNA sequencing links expression profiles to interventions on specific genes<sup>[43](#ref-43)</sup>, but control and perturbed conditions generally contain different measured cells. Measurements at successive time points reveal changes in expression distributions, while connections between individual cells’ earlier and later states must be inferred<sup>[44](#ref-44)</sup>.

## Computational models of gene regulation

Computational modeling of gene regulation has developed along two largely separate directions, focused on cellular state and regulatory sequence (**Figure 2a,b**). We review their applications and the evidence for their ability to predict molecular activity and perturbation responses across sequences and cellular conditions.

### Cell state models

Cell state models use molecular profiles to characterize cell types<sup>[45](#ref-45),[46](#ref-46)</sup>, compare cellular states across datasets and conditions<sup>[47](#ref-47),[48](#ref-48)</sup>, impute missing measurements<sup>[49](#ref-49),[50](#ref-50)</sup>, and predict responses to interventions<sup>[51](#ref-51),[52](#ref-52)</sup>. Due to the abundance of single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data, most models learn from transcriptomic profiles of individual cells, capturing coordinated expression patterns associated with cell identity and physiological state. Many recent cell state models learn latent representations (*i.e.*, embeddings) by reconstructing cellular profiles, predicting masked expression values, or using contrastive objectives that bring selected pairs of related profiles closer in representation space while separating other pairs<sup>[53](#ref-53)–[55](#ref-55)</sup>. Transcriptomic profiles provide an indirect view of the regulatory environment, while regulatory DNA and regulator protein abundance and activity generally remain implicit (**Figure 2a**).

Comparing cellular states across datasets requires accounting for technical differences among samples. For example, scVI<sup>[47](#ref-47)</sup> uses a variational autoencoder to model RNA counts through a latent cellular representation together with library size (the total measured counts per cell) and covariates identifying experimental batch. To support transfer of cell-type annotations and comparison of disease-associated states with reference populations, scArches<sup>[48](#ref-48)</sup> uses transfer learning to map new datasets into pre-trained reference space that can be obtained using existing models like scVI. Across species, similarities among encoded proteins can help relate expression profiles whose gene repertoires differ<sup>[56](#ref-56),[57](#ref-57)</sup>. SATURN combines protein representations with expression to learn weighted gene groups, termed macrogenes, that provide shared features for comparing cell types across species<sup>[58](#ref-58)</sup>.

Single-cell foundation models (scFMs) aim to learn representations that support multiple downstream tasks by learning expression relationships across broad collections of scRNA-seq data<sup>[53](#ref-53)</sup>. Pretrained representations can place newly measured cells alongside characterized reference populations or retrieve similar cells from other studies in a zero-shot fashion without updating model parameters, helping identify cell types and states that recur across tissues or conditions<sup>[56](#ref-56),[59](#ref-59)</sup>. For supervised tasks, pretrained embeddings can serve as fixed inputs to a task-specific predictor, or the model can be adapted through fine-tuning, which updates some or all pretrained parameters for the new task. Geneformer and scGPT illustrate this transfer to cell type annotation, with Geneformer also supporting disease-state classification<sup>[60](#ref-60),[61](#ref-61)</sup>.

The value of pretraining depends on the additional data and how the pretrained model is used. Controlled comparisons found that self-supervised pretraining on auxiliary datasets improved cell type annotation relative to training on the target dataset alone<sup>[62](#ref-62)</sup>. In evaluations without fine-tuning, Geneformer and scGPT embeddings showed no consistent advantage over scVI and simpler approaches fitted to the evaluation datasets for cell type separation and batch integration<sup>[63](#ref-63)</sup>. Increasing pretraining dataset size also produced diminishing gains in the tested scFMs, with plateaus varying by architecture and downstream task<sup>[64](#ref-64)</sup>.

Paired RNA and chromatin accessibility measurements<sup>[65](#ref-65)</sup> extend cellular representations by relating expression patterns to the accessibility of regulatory elements. Models can learn shared representations from these measurements, allowing information from jointly profiled cells to support integration and imputation in datasets where one modality is missing<sup>[66](#ref-66)</sup>. Pretraining directly on accessibility profiles also provides representations for cell annotation and imputation<sup>[67](#ref-67)</sup>. These approaches incorporate measured variation in the chromatin environment while generally leaving the underlying regulatory sequences implicit.

Representations learned from observational data describe variation among cells, while perturbation measurements reveal how interventions change their expression programs. Perturbation-response models can be trained directly on intervention measurements<sup>[52](#ref-52)</sup> or adapted from pretrained models using intervention identities and measured responses<sup>[61](#ref-61)</sup>. Models use different strategies for predictions beyond the interventions measured during training. For example, in CPA learned representations of constituent interventions are combined to predict new combinations<sup>[68](#ref-68)</sup>. In GEARS, prior knowledge of functional similarity of genes is used to relate genes not perturbed during training to genes with measured responses<sup>[52](#ref-52),[69](#ref-69)</sup>. scFMs seek to exploit relationships learned during large-scale pretraining<sup>[61](#ref-61)</sup>.

In a benchmark comparing perturbed-gene representations, embeddings from functional protein interaction networks supported more accurate response predictions than embeddings from the tested scFMs and protein language models<sup>[70](#ref-70)</sup>. However, recent benchmarks have challenged claims of generalization to unseen genetic perturbations, finding that the evaluated deep learning models did not consistently outperform simple baselines that assign the same training-derived mean expression profile to unseen gene perturbations or predict unseen combinations by summing individual perturbation effects relative to control<sup>[71](#ref-71),[72](#ref-72)</sup>. In these evaluations, metrics could reward expression changes shared across interventions, producing favorable scores even when intervention-specific responses were poorly predicted<sup>[72](#ref-72)</sup>. Positive-control analyses using measured responses showed that metric sensitivity affects these comparisons, with gains for some deep learning models becoming apparent when evaluation resolved perturbation-specific changes<sup>[73](#ref-73)</sup>.

A complementary challenge is predicting the effects of the same intervention across different cellular states. The State model uses cellular embeddings learned from observational data to support response prediction in contexts for which no perturbation measurements were supplied during training<sup>[74](#ref-74)</sup>. Within a given context, responses can also vary among cells, and predicting a mean profile leaves this variation unresolved. Distributional approaches aim to capture these differences by learning transformations between control and perturbed expression distributions<sup>[75](#ref-75),[76](#ref-76)</sup>. Because single-cell assays generally measure different cells in each condition, the training data constrain population responses while leaving individual-cell transitions unobserved<sup>[77](#ref-77)</sup>.

### Sequence-based models

Supervised seq2func models are trained to predict molecular measurements or functional annotations from DNA sequence (**Figure 2b**). Here, “function” refers to the molecular activity or property being modeled. Once trained, models can predict these properties for unmeasured sequences, supporting variant-effect prediction<sup>[78](#ref-78),[79](#ref-79)</sup>, *in silico* experiments to interrogate the *cis*-regulatory syntax they have learned<sup>[1](#ref-1),[80](#ref-80)–[82](#ref-82)</sup>, and the design of regulatory sequences with specified predicted activities<sup>[83](#ref-83)–[85](#ref-85)</sup>.

Single-task seq2func models predict a specified molecular readout in one cellular context, with training objectives tailored to the assay. For example, a reporter-activity task predicts the expression of a reporter gene driven by each tested regulatory sequence in a massively parallel reporter assay<sup>[86](#ref-86)–[88](#ref-88)</sup>. Models trained on TF-binding or chromatin-accessibility profiles can predict both the total signal and its distribution across nucleotide positions. These profiles provide supervision on where signal occurs relative to sequence features, information that is lost when each region is reduced to a binary label or aggregate count<sup>[81](#ref-81),[89](#ref-89)</sup>. The measured profiles also reflect technical processes with their own sequence dependencies. ChromBPNet, for example, uses a separately trained model of the sequence preferences of Tn5 in ATAC-seq or DNase I in DNase-seq to distinguish assay bias from sequence contributions associated with chromatin accessibility<sup>[90](#ref-90)</sup>.

Multi-task seq2func models share sequence representations across assays or cellular contexts, with task-specific outputs predicting each molecular readout<sup>[91](#ref-91),[92](#ref-92)</sup>. A TF-binding motif may inform predictions of TF occupancy, chromatin accessibility, and histone modifications, allowing evidence from several assays to shape a shared sequence representation. Basenji extended multi-task sequence modeling to quantitative epigenomic profiles and cap analysis of gene expression (CAGE) measurements along the genome<sup>[93](#ref-93)</sup>. Subsequent models, including Enformer<sup>[94](#ref-94)</sup>, Borzoi<sup>[95](#ref-95)</sup>, and AlphaGenome<sup>[79](#ref-79)</sup>, expanded the sequence context to make distal regulatory elements available for predicting target-gene expression. Single-cell atlases also provide training targets for sequence-only models. Decima predicts gene expression across cell types and conditions from DNA surrounding each gene<sup>[96](#ref-96)</sup>.

Genomic language models (gLMs) learn from genomes by reconstructing masked sequence or predicting successive nucleotides or nucleotide groups from preceding sequence<sup>[97](#ref-97),[98](#ref-98)</sup>. Training across species can capture patterns of evolutionary constraint<sup>[99](#ref-99)</sup>, with some models using alignments to relate positions across homologous sequences<sup>[100](#ref-100),[101](#ref-101)</sup>. These patterns can inform variant interpretation, particularly in coding and splice-associated regions<sup>[99](#ref-99),[100](#ref-100)</sup>. Connecting them to regulatory activity in a specified cellular context generally requires functional supervision, supplied through fine-tuning<sup>[102](#ref-102)</sup> or combined with continued sequence prediction, as in Nucleotide Transformer v3<sup>[103](#ref-103)</sup>. In regulatory benchmarks, the tested frozen or fine-tuned gLMs did not consistently outperform seq2func models trained directly on functional measurements<sup>[104](#ref-104),[105](#ref-105)</sup>.

For seq2func models trained on reference-genome profiles, variant-effect prediction tests whether sequence–activity relationships learned across loci transfer to differences between alleles. ChromBPNet and CREsted predictions agree well with measured effects at chromatin accessibility quantitative trait loci (caQTLs) and with allele-specific accessibility differences<sup>[90](#ref-90),[106](#ref-106)</sup>. In personal-genome expression benchmarks, however, models can achieve high correlations across genes within an individual yet struggle to predict differences among individuals for the same gene, sometimes predicting genetic effects in the wrong direction<sup>[107](#ref-107)–[109](#ref-109)</sup>.

For variants in distal regulatory elements, predicting expression effects also requires learning which genes those elements regulate and how strongly they contribute to expression<sup>[110](#ref-110),[111](#ref-111)</sup>. Earlier benchmarks found that Enformer captured many promoter determinants but underestimated distal enhancer effects<sup>[112](#ref-112)</sup>. Recent benchmarks show that improved enhancer–gene link identification can coexist with inaccurate estimates of regulatory effects. AlphaGenome identified enhancer–gene links more accurately than Borzoi<sup>[79](#ref-79)</sup>. Predicted expression changes from *in silico* enhancer disruption in AlphaGenome and other tested models were substantially smaller than those measured by CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) at experimentally supported links, with larger discrepancies at greater enhancer–promoter distances<sup>[113](#ref-113)</sup>. AlphaGenome’s predictions of expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) effects likewise became less accurate with increasing distance from the target transcription start site<sup>[79](#ref-79)</sup>.

Sharing sequence features across cellular contexts introduces tradeoffs in both predictive accuracy and interpretation. In chromatin accessibility models, strong predictions at broadly accessible regions can mask poorer performance at cell-type-specific regions<sup>[114](#ref-114)</sup>. Shared representations can also introduce *feature leakage*. A motif learned from accessible regions in one cell type can acquire a negative contribution in another because it helps distinguish their accessibility patterns, even when the TF associated with the motif is absent from the second cell type. Interpreting that contribution as repression by the TF would assign a biological mechanism to an association learned across training tasks. In a CREsted model, positive E-box contributions in interneurons and negative contributions in excitatory neurons were consistent with this failure mode<sup>[115](#ref-115)</sup>. Training separate models removes this route for transferring feature associations across cellular outputs, while forgoing shared learning across tasks.

## Joint sequence and cell state models

Joint sequence and cell state models connect regulation at individual loci with expression programs across genes. **Sequence-informed cell state models** use features of regulatory DNA to constrain regulatory relationships or predict expression from cellular measurements. **Context-aware seq2func models** condition predictions from nucleotide sequence on measurements or representations of cellular state (**Figure 2c,d**). A goal of joint modeling is to predict how sequence effects change with cellular state and how regulatory sequence shapes responses to regulator perturbations. Learning these dependencies could support predictions for sequence–context combinations that have not been measured together.

One use of sequence information is to constrain gene regulatory networks (GRNs), which describe relationships between regulators and their targets. Co-expression programs and regulator–target associations inferred from expression nominate relationships<sup>[116](#ref-116),[117](#ref-117)</sup>, while potential TF-binding sites provide sequence evidence for candidate connections. SCENIC uses motif enrichment to refine expression-derived relationships into regulons comprising a TF and its candidate target genes, and scores their activity through enrichment of those targets among highly expressed genes in each cell<sup>[118](#ref-118)</sup>. Chromatin accessibility extends this framework through candidate regulatory elements. Motif matches in accessible regions nominate TF–element links, while proximity and associations between accessibility and expression support element–gene links<sup>[14](#ref-14),[15](#ref-15),[119](#ref-119)</sup>.

Some GRN approaches also fit predictive relationships between regulators, regulatory elements, and target-gene expression. Pando models products of TF expression and accessibility at motif-containing regions, allowing the modeled contribution of a TF to depend on both its expression and accessibility at its candidate binding sites<sup>[120](#ref-120)</sup>. DeepSCENIC learns TF–element binding potentials from pretrained sequence representations and combines them with TF expression to predict accessibility and target-gene expression<sup>[16](#ref-16)</sup>. In these approaches, sequence helps specify potential regulatory connections, while cellular measurements help estimate their contributions to expression.

Fitted networks can also be used to predict how regulator perturbations affect expression across genes. CellOracle propagates an imposed change in TF expression through a motif-constrained network fitted to unperturbed cells and projects the predicted expression changes onto a cell-state representation to estimate the direction of state shifts<sup>[121](#ref-121)</sup>. Its nomination of *lhx1a* as a regulator of axial mesoderm development was supported by loss-of-function experiments in zebrafish.

Sequence-informed expression prediction also extends to models that integrate features across regulatory elements without prescribing a regulator–target network. GET combines motif-based sequence features with cell-specific chromatin accessibility to predict expression<sup>[122](#ref-122)</sup>. Here, cellular context is supplied through the measured state of regulatory elements, allowing sequence-informed prediction without TF expression as an input.

Context-aware seq2func models incorporate cellular information directly into the mapping from nucleotide sequence to molecular measurements. Corgi uses regulator expression to condition predictions of expression and epigenomic profiles<sup>[13](#ref-13)</sup>, while scooby uses learned representations of cellular state to condition predictions of RNA and chromatin accessibility profiles<sup>[12](#ref-12)</sup>. Both approaches allow sequence features to be shared across cellular states while their predicted effects depend on the supplied context. Corgi predicts molecular measurements in held-out cell types. Its variant-effect predictions agreed more closely with measured effects when regulator-expression inputs matched the cellular context in which those effects were assayed than when the inputs were mismatched<sup>[13](#ref-13)</sup>. Scooby’s variant predictions correlated more strongly than those of the sequence-only model Borzoi with fine-mapped eQTL effects in the cell types profiled by OneK1K<sup>[12](#ref-12),[123](#ref-123)</sup>, although differences in architecture and training prevent attributing that improvement specifically to context.

AlphaGenome 2 expands the training scale and biological coverage of this approach, accepting either learned embeddings for reference tissues and cell types or embeddings generated from supplied single-cell RNA-seq profiles<sup>[124](#ref-124)</sup>. On TenK10K single-cell eQTL benchmarks, it improved identification of regulatory variants and prediction of effect direction relative to the original AlphaGenome fine-tuned on single-cell data and to scooby<sup>[124](#ref-124)</sup>.

Accessibility, regulator expression, and learned cell representations can capture coordinated variation across cells, making them useful for activity prediction even when the contributions of individual regulators remain poorly resolved. Corgi’s authors reported limited sensitivity to individual regulator-input changes and did not obtain accurate zero-shot predictions in the regulator-perturbation evaluations they examined<sup>[13](#ref-13)</sup>. For networks fitted to unperturbed cells, predicting intervention responses requires the inferred regulator–target relationships to remain predictive when a regulator is perturbed. Evaluations across cellular states and regulator perturbations probe these different capabilities.

Across these approaches, predictive capabilities depend on what cellular inputs measure, which distinctions their representations preserve, and how those inputs modify predictions from sequence. We next examine how **context measurements**, sequence and context **representations**, and their **fusion** shape these predictions (**Figure 3**; **Table 1**).

### Selecting measurements of cellular context

Measurements of cellular context capture different aspects of the regulatory environment. Transcriptome profiles are widely used and provide information shared across loci through regulator expression and the expression programs associated with regulator activity<sup>[125](#ref-125)</sup>. Regulator RNA abundance can diverge from protein abundance and activity, particularly during stimulation when these quantities can change on different timescales. Protein measurements provide more direct information about abundance, although low-abundance regulators, including many TFs, remain incompletely sampled by single-cell proteomic methods<sup>[126](#ref-126)</sup>.

Epigenomic measurements complement this cell-wide information by describing the regulatory state at individual loci. Chromatin accessibility is widely measured, while histone modifications and other chromatin features provide additional information about regulatory activity and can retain features of earlier stimulation<sup>[127](#ref-127),[128](#ref-128)</sup>. Combining transcriptome and chromatin measurements can help distinguish cellular conditions that are difficult to resolve from either modality alone. Even jointly, these modalities provide a partial description of cellular state, with many aspects of regulator activity and signaling represented only indirectly.

The choice of context also determines which molecular quantities are supplied and which the model must predict. Expression models can condition predictions from promoter and enhancer sequences on measured enhancer accessibility<sup>[129](#ref-129)</sup>. Accessibility then supplies information about the outcome of TF binding and chromatin remodeling, allowing the model to use this regulatory state when predicting expression. For sequence perturbations, however, holding accessibility fixed can omit expression effects mediated through changes in accessibility. The appropriate context thus depends on whether the goal is to predict expression given a measured chromatin state or to predict how a perturbation changes that state and its downstream consequences.

### Sequence and context representations

The same cellular measurements can be supplied to a model in different forms, preserving different levels of detail. Regulator expression vectors retain a separate value for each included gene<sup>[13](#ref-13),[130](#ref-130),[131](#ref-131)</sup>, while learned cell embeddings summarize patterns across expression or accessibility profiles<sup>[12](#ref-12),[132](#ref-132)</sup>. These representations provide context shared across loci. Local chromatin measurements can remain organized as genomic signal tracks or be summarized over regulatory elements<sup>[129](#ref-129),[133](#ref-133)</sup>. Tracks preserve spatial variation at the supplied resolution, while regional summaries retain selected properties of each element.

Cell embeddings learned independently of the sequence-prediction task may preserve broad differences between cell types while poorly resolving variation within a cell type that affects regulatory responses. A fixed embedding supplies the sequence predictor with only the cellular distinctions it retains. XChrom, for example, learns a transformation of fixed principal component analysis (PCA) coordinates of expression profiles<sup>[134](#ref-134)</sup>. This transformation can adapt how the retained components contribute to accessibility prediction, but cannot recover distinctions removed by the original projection. Training the encoder that maps the original measurements into a cell representation jointly with the sequence predictor allows the prediction objective to influence which cellular differences are retained.

The sequence representation determines which regulatory features are available to combine with this cellular context. Regional motif summaries, as used by GET, retain motif content while omitting the arrangement of sites within each region<sup>[122](#ref-122)</sup>. Features learned by seq2func models can encode relationships such as motif order and spacing. Pooling aggregates features across sequence positions into a coarser representation; these relationships can survive pooling if they have already been encoded in the aggregated features. Their retention depends on the encoder and the measurements and cellular conditions used for training. Features computed from DNA alone are shared across cellular states, with context-dependent effects modeled when sequence and context representations are combined. For variant prediction, motif-derived features or sequence embeddings must be recomputed from the altered DNA. Sequence changes that produce identical representations remain indistinguishable through that input.

Compressing sequence into a predicted activity value can discard distinctions needed under a different context. Two enhancers with different TF-binding motifs may have the same predicted accessibility in one state yet respond differently to altered TF activity. Passing only their initial accessibility values to a downstream predictor collapses this distinction, even if those values are accurate. Retaining sequence features alongside the activity estimate, or recomputing accessibility from sequence under the altered context, allows motif composition to contribute to the response prediction.

### Fusion of sequence and context representations

Fusion specifies how a model uses cellular context to interpret regulatory sequence. A sequence representation may identify a potential TF-binding site, while the supplied cellular measurements provide information about the availability or activity of the corresponding regulator. Combining these inputs allows the predicted contribution of that site, and the effect of disrupting it, to vary across cellular states.

Two design choices describe this integration. The *fusion stage* specifies where context acts in the prediction pipeline. Early fusion introduces context at the input, intermediate fusion introduces it within the sequence encoder, and late fusion introduces it at the prediction head, the final layers that map encoded features to predicted molecular measurements (**Figure 3**; **Table 1**). The *fusion operation* specifies how context changes feature processing or prediction. A model can apply several operations at different stages, allowing context to influence both the construction of sequence features and their contribution to the output.

Concatenation places sequence and context features alongside one another as inputs to subsequent layers. In early fusion, local chromatin measurements can be aligned with DNA as additional input channels<sup>[133](#ref-133),[135](#ref-135)</sup>. The encoder receives nucleotide information and measured signal at corresponding positions. Subsequent nonlinear layers can learn dependencies between sequence patterns and the surrounding chromatin state, such as how the predictive contribution of a motif varies with local accessibility. Concatenation can also be applied at the prediction head, combining encoded sequence features with cellular context for expression prediction, as in DeepCAGE<sup>[131](#ref-131)</sup>.

Multiplication provides a direct interaction between sequence and context features. Weighting a sequence-derived binding score by regulator expression makes the resulting feature depend on both the binding potential and the supplied regulator level<sup>[136](#ref-136)</sup>. Gating generalizes this approach through context-dependent scaling of features, while feature-wise linear modulation (FiLM) learns coefficients that both scale and shift them<sup>[137](#ref-137)</sup>. Corgi applies FiLM within the sequence encoder, using regulator expression to modulate features at multiple stages<sup>[13](#ref-13)</sup>. Later layers process these modified features, allowing cellular context to influence how sequence patterns are combined during encoding. AlphaGenome 2 similarly applies FiLM throughout its sequence encoder, transformer tower, and decoder<sup>[124](#ref-124)</sup>. Conditioning only the decoder or output layers did not consistently improve accuracy over an unconditioned multi-task model. Prediction of expression differences across conditions improved when conditioning extended to the transformer, with further gains from including the sequence encoder<sup>[124](#ref-124)</sup>.

Cross-attention allows different sequence regions to use different parts of the supplied context. When context contains separate regulator representations, sequence features can guide how strongly each regulator representation contributes at a particular region<sup>[138](#ref-138)</sup>. A cell-wide context can thus be used differently across loci according to their sequence features.

Hypernetworks use context to generate parameters for another component of the model<sup>[139](#ref-139)</sup>. In scooby, a hypernetwork generates output-layer weights from the cell embedding, implementing late fusion through a cell-dependent prediction head<sup>[12](#ref-12)</sup>. The same encoded sequence can consequently produce different predictions across cells. This approach allows sequence features to be reused across contexts, while requiring the shared encoder to preserve the distinctions needed by the different prediction heads.

## Learning and evaluating context-dependent regulatory relationships

Training data constrain which sequence–context relationships a model can learn. Assay design, sampling, and preprocessing shape the comparisons available to distinguish these relationships, while evaluation tests whether the resulting predictions transfer to new sequences and cellular conditions.

### Training across datasets and modalities

Joint models can learn sequence and cell-state representations together with the fusion and prediction layers from data that link regulatory DNA and cellular context to a molecular readout. Separate pretraining extends the data available to each component. A sequence encoder can learn regulatory features from DNA paired with genome-wide functional measurements<sup>[94](#ref-94),[95](#ref-95)</sup>, while a cell-state encoder can learn expression patterns from independently collected transcriptomic atlases<sup>[60](#ref-60)</sup>. Joint supervision still requires examples that link the supplied context to molecular activity at the corresponding locus. The pretrained encoders can remain fixed while the fusion and prediction layers are fitted, or be fine-tuned alongside them.

For accessibility prediction conditioned on RNA, paired assays associate accessibility at a locus with the expression profile of the same cell or nucleus<sup>[65](#ref-65)</sup>. Applying these assays to perturbed and control populations preserves that correspondence within cells, while intervention responses are estimated by comparing populations<sup>[163](#ref-163),[164](#ref-164)</sup>. When assays profile different cells, matching populations across assays introduces uncertainty about which expression context belongs with an accessibility measurement. Pooling studies can broaden biological coverage, but even correctly matched profiles may carry protocol effects that survive normalization. If protocol coincides with cell type or intervention, the model can learn these differences as apparent context-dependent regulation<sup>[165](#ref-165),[166](#ref-166)</sup>. Shared samples or biological conditions assayed across protocols help estimate these effects and assess whether correction preserves the regulatory differences of interest.

Constructing metacell or pseudobulk profiles within donors, conditions, and cell populations reduces sparsity while preserving comparisons among genetic backgrounds and interventions<sup>[167](#ref-167),[168](#ref-168)</sup>. Even within these groups, pooling responding and unresponsive cells can dilute a response confined to a subset. For changes retained at that resolution, an auxiliary loss can penalize errors in predicted differences from matched controls or between intervention responses. This gives response errors explicit weight alongside errors in predicted expression or accessibility levels.

### Experiments that resolve sequence–context dependencies

Cellular states differ in the expression and activity of many regulators, so a sequence’s response across states can reflect several regulatory changes acting together. Reporter libraries assayed across cell types measure responses to these different environments<sup>[87](#ref-87)</sup>. To test dependence on a particular regulator, intact and motif-disrupted sequences can be compared at several TF dosages. Their activity difference at each dosage estimates the edit’s effect; comparing that difference across dosages measures how the sequence effect changes with regulator availability. Repeating these comparisons across cellular backgrounds tests whether other features of the state modify the dependency. At endogenous loci, crossing sequence edits with regulator interventions would test whether a gene’s response to the intervention depends on the edited regulatory sequence.

Sampling a range of regulator activity can expose sequence features whose contributions are weak under baseline conditions. In cranial neural crest cells, models fine-tuned on accessibility responses to TF dosage identified low-affinity TWIST1 motifs associated with dosage sensitivity despite weak contributions to predicted accessibility in unperturbed cells. Reporter assays supported their role in increasing sensitivity to changes in TWIST1 dosage<sup>[169](#ref-169)</sup>. Interpreting differences in sensitivity across cellular contexts requires measuring the achieved change in regulator abundance or activity and recording when the molecular measurements were collected after intervention. Otherwise, unequal perturbation strengths or sampling times can appear as differences in regulatory sensitivity.

Varying binding-site affinity or TF dosage characterizes a particular sequence–regulator dependency over a broader range. Distinguishing the contributions of correlated regulatory components requires comparisons that vary those components separately. Perturbing coexpressed TFs individually and disrupting motifs that frequently co-occur can help distinguish contributions that remain unresolved in observational measurements<sup>[170](#ref-170)</sup>. Combined perturbations then test how those contributions change when other components are altered, including cases where joint perturbations produce substantial responses despite little effect from the individual interventions<sup>[171](#ref-171)</sup>.

### Evaluating regulatory effects

#### Predicting regulatory changes

Regulatory-effect evaluation compares predicted and measured changes at the same genes or loci across conditions. Scores pooled across genes or loci can be dominated by their persistent differences in baseline activity, even when changes across conditions are poorly predicted. In PanExonNet benchmarks, models with high overall exon-inclusion accuracy differed substantially in predicting deviations from each exon’s median inclusion across training samples<sup>[159](#ref-159)</sup>. Predicting each gene or locus’s average activity across training contexts establishes how much performance is explained by this stable component. Reusing the profile of the training context most similar in the supplied cellular measurements tests whether the model improves on a similar observed state<sup>[172](#ref-172)</sup>. Reporting performance at differentially active loci alongside results across all loci then exposes errors specifically in context-dependent changes<sup>[114](#ref-114)</sup>.

In the crossed motif-edit and TF-dosage experiment, the interaction contrast is the change in the edit’s effect between dosages. Sequence-only and context-only models indicate how well either input predicts activity. A model that sums separate sequence and context contributions predicts a constant edit effect on the scale where they are added. Comparing the measured contrast with predictions from the joint and additive models tests whether joint modeling improves prediction of dosage-dependent edit effects. The edit effects at each dosage and the dosage effects for each sequence should also be evaluated because errors in those effects can cancel in the contrast.

Interpreting this contrast as regulatory cooperation requires specifying the response scale, such as linear or log-transformed expression. Saturation can make a combined increase in measured activity smaller than the sum of the individual increases, even when the underlying contributions are additive<sup>[173](#ref-173)</sup>. A baseline fitted to training data can test how much of the measured interaction is explained by a shared nonlinear mapping from summed contributions to the readout. Dose series help constrain the shape of that response function.

For transcriptome-wide responses, predicting which genes respond to an intervention and estimating the magnitude of their expression changes are distinct tasks. Correlation across genes can remain high when effect sizes are systematically underestimated. Comparing predicted and experimentally identified responders across all evaluated genes tests which responses the model detects or misses. Reporting effect-size errors among measured responders then exposes errors otherwise obscured by agreement on unchanged expression<sup>[174](#ref-174)</sup>. Even among responding genes, different interventions can elicit similar changes. Predicting the mean change from controls across training interventions in a comparable cell population tests how much of the response can be predicted without distinguishing the interventions<sup>[72](#ref-72)</sup>.

Measured responses provide an empirical check on the metric itself. The mean expression change from matched controls in one group of perturbed cells can serve as a prediction for another group exposed to the same intervention<sup>[73](#ref-73)</sup>. Comparing its score with that of a different intervention’s response tests whether the metric rewards intervention-specific information. These controls use observed responses and should be reported separately from training-derived baselines. Splitting cells from one sample measures within-sample consistency, while comparisons across independent biological replicates measure reproducibility<sup>[175](#ref-175)</sup>.

A population-level response includes changes in expression within cellular states and changes in the proportions of those states. The mean expression profile reflects both contributions, allowing errors in one to compensate for errors in the other. Where corresponding states can be identified, evaluating within-state expression distributions separately from state proportions distinguishes these sources of the response<sup>[168](#ref-168)</sup>. This assessment must also retain states that appear or disappear after intervention, even when their absence from one condition prevents a within-state expression comparison.

#### Generalization and adaptation

Holding out a sequence–state combination while observing both the sequence and the state elsewhere in training tests whether a model can predict a new combination of familiar inputs. Withholding entire loci, regulator interventions, donors, or cellular states tests transfer along each of those axes<sup>[176](#ref-176)</sup>. For genomic holdouts, evaluation should distinguish exposure to test sequences during pretraining from access to their molecular readouts during training or through cellular inputs. For example, scooby excludes genes and accessibility peaks overlapping validation and test regions before constructing its cell embeddings<sup>[12](#ref-12)</sup>. Using measurements from a new locus to fit enhancer–gene weights, or from a new study to fine-tune a model, evaluates adaptation. When predictions are available before adaptation, comparing the original and adapted models on the same independent test samples measures the gain from those additional data.

The degree of extrapolation can vary substantially among holdouts of the same kind. A held-out cell type may closely resemble training cell types in regulator expression or combine familiar regulator levels in a previously unobserved pattern. Along a developmental trajectory, withholding an intermediate stage while retaining earlier and later stages can test interpolation. Forecasting beyond the observed time range tests temporal extrapolation, while a held-out lineage may share early states with training lineages and diverge later. RNA measured at a held-out stage can supply context for predicting accessibility at that stage. Forecasting how cells reach that state after an intervention requires the starting measurements and the specified intervention.

Genomic holdouts also span different degrees of distribution shift. Donor haplotypes can carry single-nucleotide substitutions and structural variants whose frequencies differ among populations, while transfer across species can involve broader changes in sequence composition and motif organization. Shifts in the distribution of sequence or cellular inputs constitute *covariate shift* when the relationship between those inputs and regulatory activity remains stable<sup>[177](#ref-177)</sup>. *Concept shift* occurs when the distribution of activity changes at the same sequence and measured cellular context. For example, stimulation can alter TF activity and target-gene transcription at unchanged regulator RNA levels.

Across studies, differences in cell-type proportions can change aggregate prediction scores even when accuracy within each type remains stable. For cell-type classification, this constitutes *label shift* if the distribution of measured profiles within each type is preserved<sup>[178](#ref-178)</sup>. For expression and accessibility prediction, assay protocols can also change the scale or variability of the readout under matched biological conditions<sup>[165](#ref-165)</sup>. A shift in signal scale can bias predicted effect sizes, while greater measurement noise can reduce agreement even when mean predictions remain accurate. Shared samples profiled across studies help distinguish these measurement effects from biological differences. Within and across studies, reporting regulatory-effect errors by variant class, regulator dosage relative to the training range, and similarity to profiled cellular states can reveal where predictions deteriorate under shifts relevant to the intended application. For models reporting prediction intervals, coverage and width within each group can expose overconfidence in unfamiliar states that aggregate coverage would obscure<sup>[179](#ref-179),[180](#ref-180)</sup>.

## Interpreting regulatory relationships across sequence and cellular state

Model interpretation aims to characterize the relationships learned by a predictive model and use them to formulate testable hypotheses about biological regulation. Ante hoc approaches encourage interpretable representations through model design, including architectures designed to represent sequence motifs or gene modules<sup>[181](#ref-181)–[186](#ref-186)</sup>. Here, we focus on post hoc approaches that nominate regulatory features, test their necessity and sufficiency within the model, and quantify their interactions. For joint models, these approaches can examine which sequence sites and cellular inputs explain why a predicted sequence effect changes across states.

*Attribution analysis* assigns scores to input features, such as nucleotides or regulator expression values, to describe their influence on a selected model output. Gradient saliency measures local sensitivity to input changes<sup>[187](#ref-187)</sup>, while DeepLIFT, DeepSHAP, and integrated gradients estimate contributions relative to a reference input or background distribution<sup>[188](#ref-188)–[190](#ref-190)</sup>. The reference specifies the inputs used for comparison, such as shuffled DNA sequences or control expression profiles. Attributing a cellular embedding identifies gene inputs that shape the learned representation<sup>[191](#ref-191)</sup>, while attributing a predicted expression difference from a matched control identifies inputs contributing to the modeled response.

For DNA inputs, nucleotide attributions localize candidate regulatory sites. In sequence-to-profile models, PISA resolves attributions across output positions, showing where along the predicted profile an input feature contributes<sup>[192](#ref-192)</sup>. Comparing nucleotide attribution patterns across loci can identify recurring motifs. TF-MoDISco identifies short sequence segments with strong attribution signals, termed seqlets, and aligns and clusters their attribution patterns to discover motifs<sup>[193](#ref-193)</sup>. TF-MInDi represents seqlets through their similarities to a reference motif collection, clusters them in that representation, and constructs consensus motifs from the resulting groups<sup>[194](#ref-194)</sup>.

Controlled *in silico experiments* test how a prediction or learned representation responds to changes in its inputs. In Geneformer, deleting a gene token or raising its rank in the encoded expression profile tests how cell or gene embeddings depend on that gene<sup>[60](#ref-60)</sup>. In seq2func models, disrupting or introducing a candidate site can test whether it is necessary or sufficient for predicted activity in a specified sequence background. A motif knockout tests necessity by disrupting the site in its original context and measuring the change in predicted expression, accessibility, or TF binding while holding cellular inputs fixed. Single-nucleotide mutagenesis resolves which substitutions disrupt or preserve the site’s contribution.

Motif knock-ins test sufficiency by asking whether introducing the site into another background can confer the predicted activity or response of interest. Because the receiving sequence may supply different supporting or competing features, an implantation effect is specific to that background. Global importance analysis (GIA) extends this test across a chosen background distribution, averaging prediction differences between matched sequences with and without the implanted feature<sup>[80](#ref-80)</sup>. Varying motif identity, copy number, or flanking sequence can then reveal which properties account for differences in the response. This distinguishes contributions associated with the core motif from those that require a particular local sequence context. DeepSTARR analyses and reporter assays examined this distinction by exchanging flanks between weak and strong GATA motif instances, finding that the exchanges could alter enhancer activity while retaining the core motif<sup>[86](#ref-86)</sup>.

Mutations around a reference sequence can reveal how disrupting one site changes the contributions of others. Second-order *in silico* mutagenesis measures these dependencies through departures of double-mutant effects from the sum of individual effects on the chosen output scale. DFIM measures how perturbing a nucleotide or motif changes attribution to other sites, identifying changes in their contributions to the prediction<sup>[195](#ref-195)</sup>. SEAM extends this comparison to whole attribution maps across variants, grouping similar patterns and relating them to sequence changes<sup>[196](#ref-196)</sup>. These groups can reveal alternative motif configurations or increased contributions from weak or cryptic sites, including cases where changes in feature use produce little change in predicted activity. SQUID summarizes predictions across a local variant library with an *interpretable surrogate*, a simpler model fitted to the original predictor’s outputs to estimate nucleotide contributions and optional pairwise interactions<sup>[197](#ref-197)</sup>. A nonlinear mapping from the fitted sequence contributions to the predicted readout can account for saturation separately from nucleotide-specific interaction terms<sup>[198](#ref-198)</sup>.

Designed combinations of motif or element perturbations can then test specific hypotheses about cooperation and compensation. Implanting two motifs individually and together asks whether their joint effect can be explained by their separate contributions, while varying their spacing tests whether the dependency requires a particular arrangement. BPNet analyses used such comparisons to quantify how one motif altered predicted TF binding at another across different spacings<sup>[81](#ref-81)</sup>. At the scale of regulatory elements, compensation can conceal a region’s contribution when another element remains intact. CREME searches for influential combinations through systematic perturbations of sequence tiles, contiguous segments of the input DNA<sup>[82](#ref-82)</sup>. Shuffling tiles in their genomic context tests necessity, while retaining selected tiles and the promoter at their original positions in shuffled backgrounds tests sufficiency. Comparing individual tile effects with the effects of combinations selected by the search can reveal contributions masked by compensation or nominate cooperative relationships within the model.

In joint models, attribution methods and *in silico* perturbations can examine how cellular inputs modify the predicted effect of a sequence variant. Comparing sequence attributions for the same DNA across states, using the same attribution baseline, can nominate sites whose contributions vary with context. Attributing the predicted variant–reference difference to cellular inputs can then nominate contextual features for testing. When TF expression is an explicit input, changing its value and recomputing the allele difference tests whether the predicted variant effect depends on the supplied TF transcript abundance.

Disrupting an additional candidate binding site in both allelic backgrounds and repeating the regulator-input comparison tests whether that site contributes to the predicted dependence of the variant effect on regulator expression. Related TFs and alternative site disruptions provide specificity tests when regulators are coexpressed or recognize similar motifs. Comparing predicted variant effects across measured cell profiles can also identify cellular backgrounds for experimental testing. Searches over unmeasured context inputs could extend these comparisons<sup>[187](#ref-187),[199](#ref-199)</sup>, although optimization can favor states in which prediction errors exaggerate the variant effect<sup>[200](#ref-200),[201](#ref-201)</sup>. Prioritizing biologically plausible states and assessing uncertainty in those contexts could guide experiments that cross the nominated sequence edits with perturbations of regulator abundance or activity.

## Discussion and outlook

A near-term priority is to test whether joint models predict sequence effects across regulator perturbations and regulator responses across sequence backgrounds. Coupling these models over time could extend prediction to the propagation and persistence of regulatory responses. Repeated scaling pilots could guide investment toward measurements that improve the targeted predictions, while agentic loops could use evaluation and interpretation to refine model designs and propose subsequent experiments.

### Coupling sequence and cell state predictions over time

Explaining how cells develop and respond to perturbations requires identifying how early regulatory changes produce later cellular states. The order and timing of molecular events help distinguish regulatory pathways that lead to similar endpoint expression profiles. A variant that increases TF protein abundance may alter transcription at the TF’s target genes according to their regulatory sequences, including genes encoding regulators that reinforce or oppose the initial response. Changes in TF activity can also alter chromatin accessibility and thereby modify subsequent transcription<sup>[34](#ref-34),[35](#ref-35)</sup>. Coupling sequence and cell state models over time could represent these dependencies explicitly, generating causal hypotheses that temporal measurements and timed perturbations could help distinguish.

Single-cell measurements collected at multiple time points provide snapshots of changing cell populations, supporting reconstruction of developmental trajectories and forward simulation of cellular responses<sup>[202](#ref-202),[203](#ref-203)</sup>. Changes in state abundance can reflect transitions between states or differences in proliferation and cell death, which models of population responses need to account for<sup>[202](#ref-202)</sup>. Models of RNA and chromatin kinetics further constrain how molecular states change<sup>[204](#ref-204),[205](#ref-205)</sup>. Incorporating regulatory interactions into RNA production, splicing, and degradation dynamics supports simulations of regulator perturbations<sup>[206](#ref-206)</sup>, while jointly modeling gene expression and motif-associated accessibility connects RNA dynamics to chromatin changes<sup>[207](#ref-207)</sup>. Coupling these formulations to sequence-dependent predictions could specify how an edit alters transcription or accessibility as the regulatory environment evolves.

A first step would couple sequence and cell state models through repeated updates (**Figure 4**). A context-aware seq2func component would predict locus-specific molecular readouts from DNA and the current cellular context. A dynamical component would estimate changes in molecular abundance and regulator activity over each interval from the current state, molecular readouts, and specified intervention. The first update would use readouts measured at *t*<sub>0</sub>. Subsequent updates would use readouts predicted from DNA and the evolving cellular state. Predicted RNA abundance or accessibility could serve as inputs to a learned state update. A kinetic model would require estimates of rates such as transcription and RNA degradation, together with a model linking those rates to the measured assay signals. The updated state would condition the next sequence prediction, so subsequent transcription would depend on the consequences of earlier regulation. An enhancer edit would remain in the sequence throughout the simulation, while a transient regulator perturbation would be imposed according to its experimental schedule. Errors in molecular abundance, activity, or timing could consequently affect later steps in the simulation.

Time-resolved perturbation measurements could constrain the intermediate states and transition times predicted by the coupled model. Connecting transcription to subsequent regulator abundance requires accounting for RNA processing and degradation, translation, and protein turnover<sup>[208](#ref-208),[209](#ref-209)</sup>. Paired RNA and protein measurements across differentiating cells have revealed RNA changes that precede protein changes along inferred trajectories<sup>[126](#ref-126)</sup>. Regulator activity can also change independently of abundance. If similar measured profiles lead to different responses because of prior stimulation, the dynamical state may need to retain information about that history<sup>[127](#ref-127)</sup>. Measurements of RNA, chromatin accessibility, and selected regulator proteins or activities throughout a response could help the model learn how these processes shape subsequent transcription.

Destructive molecular assays sample different cells at successive times, leaving the connections between measured states to be inferred. Lineage tracing can constrain relationships between earlier and later cell populations<sup>[44](#ref-44)</sup>, while linking transcriptomic snapshots to live-cell imaging can provide information about how individual cells reached those states<sup>[210](#ref-210)</sup>. Live-cell imaging could follow individual cells through a perturbation response, while transcriptomic profiling of selected cells at defined times would link their expression states to preceding behavior and reporter dynamics<sup>[211](#ref-211)</sup>. A model trained on these paired imaging histories and transcriptomes could estimate expression changes along the tracked trajectories, providing temporal information between molecular sampling times. Measured transcriptomes would constrain the trajectories at sampled times, while predictions at times withheld from training could be compared with the corresponding transcriptomes to assess which genes or expression programs are recoverable from imaging. Errors on these held-out measurements could help calibrate uncertainty in the inferred expression states, including cases where similar imaging histories accompany different molecular profiles. The inferred trajectories could then provide additional constraints on the coupled model’s state updates, with less certain estimates receiving less weight during training.

Coupled models could help identify intervention schedules that drive cells toward a desired state that persists after withdrawal. Reprogramming with the Yamanaka factors illustrates why timing matters, as the order and duration of factor expression can influence the transition to pluripotency<sup>[212](#ref-212),[213](#ref-213)</sup>. A coupled model could predict how early changes in chromatin accessibility and endogenous regulator activity alter the response to later interventions. Comparing predicted trajectories under alternative schedules could then help prioritize when to introduce or withdraw individual factors and identify intermediate regulatory changes that distinguish successful transitions from stalled or alternative responses.

Evaluation would begin each predicted trajectory from the measured starting state and specified intervention schedule, with subsequent updates conditioned on the model’s own predicted context. Comparisons with independent time-resolved measurements would assess the order, timing, and magnitude of molecular responses and whether the target state is reached and maintained. Inhibiting an intermediate regulator before a transition and after the new state is established could test its roles in initiation and maintenance. Withdrawal of the initiating stimulus, combined with perturbation of regulators predicted to sustain the response, could test whether the proposed feedback explains persistence or reversal of the cellular state.

### Data scaling and continual refinement

Scaling joint sequence–cell state models requires choosing which additional measurements will improve prediction of sequence effects and regulator responses across cellular contexts. Collecting more examples within one cellular context can refine the sequence–activity relationships learned there, while comparisons across contexts provide evidence for how those relationships change with the regulatory environment. Within one cell type, testing regulatory sites individually and in combination can reveal how the effect of disrupting one site changes when another is disrupted. Repeating selected edits across TF dosages can then show how those sequence effects vary with regulator availability. These strategies compete for experimental resources, and extending measurements to additional tissues or species introduces further costs and differences in biological coverage. Pilot scaling studies could compare how these experimental strategies improve prediction of sequence effects and regulator responses relative to their costs, providing evidence for which investments to expand.

Each pilot would train models on the existing data mixture alone and with progressively larger subsets of each candidate collection. At each data scale, the pilot would explore a range of architectures, representations, and training objectives for each collection, using common validation tasks and recording the computational costs of model selection and training. For each modeling approach, gains from the added data would be evaluated separately on held-out tasks such as predicting variant effects across cellular states and regulator responses across sequence backgrounds. This would show whether a collection improves both capabilities or primarily benefits one task under particular training objectives. For example, perturbation profiles may yield larger gains in response prediction when training gives explicit weight to expression changes from matched controls.

Pilot studies could also vary the proportions of different collections at matched experimental budgets or compare policies for selecting measurements within a collection. Active-learning policies use criteria such as uncertainty or diversity to select the next measurements as the model is updated<sup>[214](#ref-214),[215](#ref-215)</sup>, with applications to regulatory sequence and perturbation-response prediction<sup>[216](#ref-216),[217](#ref-217)</sup>. Comparing the resulting performance curves alongside data-generation and training costs could guide which collections or mixtures to expand and, where extrapolation is supported, estimate the investment needed to reach a performance target<sup>[218](#ref-218)</sup>.

Monitoring gains as collections grow would help identify when to repeat the pilots. Broad measurements across cell types may initially reveal sequence features shared across contexts. Once these features are well characterized, testing the same motif edits across TF dosages may better resolve differences in regulatory sensitivity. If assay noise obscures the remaining effects, greater measurement precision may be more valuable than broader coverage. Diminishing returns from observational pretraining in some transcriptomic models and tasks<sup>[64](#ref-64)</sup> motivate comparing these alternatives as the model and its training objective change.

Successive rounds may produce small, targeted studies that address errors left by much larger training collections. Abundant assays can dominate joint training, while updating a genome-wide model on perturbations at a few loci risks degrading predictions elsewhere through catastrophic forgetting<sup>[219](#ref-219)</sup>. Continual learning could reduce the cost of retraining after each round<sup>[5](#ref-5),[220](#ref-220)</sup>. Evaluating gains on the targeted perturbations alongside performance at previously modeled loci and cellular states would test whether an update incorporates the new evidence while retaining earlier capabilities.

### Agentic discovery and refinement of regulatory models

Agents based on large language models (LLMs) could use biological knowledge and results from previous tests to propose and refine regulatory models, explanations of model behavior, or experimental designs. FunSearch and AlphaEvolve illustrate how LLM-generated programs can be improved through automated evaluation and evolutionary search, which repeatedly generates variants of candidate programs and selects them according to their evaluation scores<sup>[221](#ref-221),[222](#ref-222)</sup>. In regulatory modeling, feedback could come from predictive performance on a validation set of perturbation measurements or from whether a proposed explanation accounts for a trained model’s responses to controlled input changes. An *agentic loop* would use this feedback to revise proposals and select subsequent tests. Experimental-design loops would receive feedback as new measurements become available, with assay cost and duration shaping the rate of iteration.

For model optimization, agents could explore alternative architectures and training procedures through an evolutionary search over candidate models<sup>[221](#ref-221),[223](#ref-223)</sup>. Each round would train and evaluate candidates against a fixed objective that measures the intended regulatory capabilities, such as predicting changes in variant effects across cellular states or responses to regulator perturbations. The search could vary where cellular context enters sequence processing, which transformations preserve information about regulator activity, or how locus-specific predictions contribute to a cellular response. A candidate model could connect predicted changes in regulator expression to transcription at downstream loci, testing whether explicitly representing regulatory cascades improves response prediction. Evaluation procedures would remain outside the agents’ control, with reserved tests assessing whether improvements extend beyond the evaluations used during search.

For model interpretation, agents could use attribution and perturbation results to formulate hypotheses about how sequence features and cellular inputs jointly affect predictions, then select *in silico* experiments that distinguish competing explanations. Changing a regulator input before and after disrupting a candidate binding site, for example, could test whether the model’s response to that input depends on the site. These comparisons would refine explanations of the trained model’s behavior without requiring new biological measurements. Prediction failures and the resulting regulatory hypotheses could then guide proposals for experiments in cells, with feasibility and cost shaping which sequences, regulators, and cellular contexts are tested. The pilot-scaling framework could compare these proposed collections with established acquisition strategies by measuring improvements in sequence-effect and regulator-response prediction at matched experimental and computational budgets.

## Glossary terms

- ***cis*-regulatory element:** is a DNA sequence that regulates transcription of a target gene on the same DNA molecule
- ***trans*-acting factor:** is a diffusible regulatory molecule, such as a protein or RNA, whose action does not require physical linkage between its encoding gene and its target gene
- **chromatin state:** is the molecular and structural properties of chromatin, including nucleosome organization, accessibility, histone modifications, DNA methylation and three-dimensional organization
- **cell state:** is the molecular and physiological condition of a cell at a given time, including its chromatin state and the abundance and activity of regulatory molecules
- **learned representation:** a numerical encoding of an input, such as a cellular profile, whose features are learned from data and used for comparisons or predictions
- **pretraining:** an initial training stage that learns model parameters or representations for reuse in subsequent tasks, either directly or after fine-tuning
- **foundation model:** a model pretrained on a broad data collection with the aim of supporting multiple tasks through its learned representations or further adaptation
- **single-task seq2func model:** a sequence-to-function model trained to predict a particular molecular readout in a specified cellular context
- **multi-task seq2func model:** a sequence-to-function model that shares learned sequence representations to predict multiple molecular readouts or the same readout across cellular contexts
- **feature leakage:** in a multi-task model, transfer of a learned feature association across outputs that produces misleading regulatory dependencies
- **fusion:** the integration of sequence and cellular context through joint processing of representations, modulation of features, or generation of context-dependent model parameters. The fusion stage specifies where context acts, and the fusion operation specifies how.
- **metacell:** an aggregate molecular profile constructed from a small group of cells with similar measured profiles to reduce sparsity
- **pseudobulk:** an aggregate profile typically constructed by summing counts across cells of a defined population within each biological sample, such as one cell type from one donor
- **covariate shift:** a change in the distribution of model inputs while the conditional relationship between inputs and prediction targets remains stable
- **concept shift:** a change in the conditional relationship between model inputs and prediction targets, potentially reflecting an unmeasured regulatory variable or a change in measurement
- **label shift:** a change in target class prevalence while the distribution of inputs within each class remains stable
- **attribution method:** a method that assigns scores to input features to describe their contributions to a selected model output or the output’s sensitivity to changes in those features
- ***in silico* experiment:** a controlled comparison of model predictions under specified input changes, used to test hypotheses about feature contributions and interactions within the model
- **agentic loop:** an iterative process in which an AI agent selects actions toward a specified objective and uses feedback from their execution to guide subsequent actions
