Summary

ChromBPNet is a deep learning model of regulatory DNA that models chromatin accessibility at base-resolution with DNase I/Tn5 transposase enzyme bias-factorization. Despite wide use of the model, there have been several improvements that we had been considering. Here, we apply those updates and present a refactored codebase of ChromBPNet, that includes the following updates and improvements: (1) updated support on modern packages and hardware, with flexibility across all 3 major deep learning frameworks: PyTorch, TensorFlow and JAX; (2) a corrected, flexible cut-site convention for DNase-seq; (3) optimizations from Anthropic’s Claude Fable/Mythos 5.1 model release for ChromBPNet’s pipeline; and (4) further optimizations in pre- and post-processing. For all updates, we confirmed reproducibility with the original model, across numerical agreement (where possible), model performance, and intepretation. We share this study to showcase how deep learning model implementations can be reliably optimized despite non-deterministic numerical identity, and highlight the benefits of early open access code to help build better software as a community.

ChromBPNet: A bias-factorized, base-resolution deep learning model of chromatin accessibility

ChromBPNet is a deep learning model of chromatin accessibility, where:

  • Chromatin: is the native state of a DNA molecule in eukaryotic cells, packaged into a dense, compact structure with other proteins, such as histones, and RNA.
  • Accessibility: quantifies how non-compact/exposed the DNA is at each position, for it to freely interact with other cellular processes, such as RNA transcription.

Differences in chromatin accessibility is a defining feature of cell type identity: different cell types can have drastically different levels of accesibility for a given position on the genome, that is typically related to its proximity towards essential genes for the given cell type.

Given the importance of chromatin accessibility, many experiments have been developed to measure chromatin accessibility across different cell types:

  • DNase-seq: uses the enzyme, DNase I, to cut exposed chromatin into small fragments, that are then sequenced to identify where the exposed regions are.
  • ATAC-seq: uses the enzyme, Tn5 transposase, to fragment accessible chromatin, that again, is sequenced to identify accessible regions.

In short: The more accessible a region of chromatin is > The more its DNA gets cut up by the enzymes > Produces more short DNA fragments > More fragments are sequenced by next generation sequencing (NGS) > The sequenced fragments are mapped to find its genomic location > The number of sequenced fragments at each position are counted, piling up as a tall “peak” in the signal > Overall, the “height” of the signal quantifies how accessible the DNA is at that position.

![Figure: DNase-seq, ATAC-seq](ChromatinAccessibility.gif “width=600 DNase-seq and ATAC-seq experiments profile regions of accessible chromatin: (1) DNA that is wrapped around proteins and RNA, such as histones, is relatively inaccessible, whereas (2) exposed DNA can be cut by enzymes: DNase-I or Tn5 transposase. These enzymes preferentially act on accessible DNA, and the resulting fragments are isolated, (3) sequenced, and mapped back to the genome. (4) Regions where many fragments accumulate appear as “peaks” of chromatin accessibility.”)

While measuring the accessibility of chromatin is the primary goal of the experiments, the enzymes–DNase I and Tn5 transposase–each have a unique DNA-cutting mechanism and an innate preference towards particular DNA sequences, that causes the observed signal to reflect a combination of both true chromatin accessibility and assay-specific artifacts. Deconvolving these two signals can be particularly important when understanding the signal at single-base resolution.

To address this issue, we developed ChromBPNet: a deep learning model trained to predict chromatin accessibility signal from DNA sequence at base-resolution, with explicit factorization of the enzyme’s artifact bias. ChromBPNet, together with robust model interpretation methods, has previously been shown to predict accessibility in unseen chromosomes, identify the binding preference and binding sites of known, cell type-specific transcription factors (TFs), and predict the effects of genomic variation on accessibility (caQTLs) across cell type and population. Further, we expanded the model across a broader range of cell types, training 1,512 ChromBPNet models across 408 samples as part of ENCODE GRAMMAR.

However, with production-level use, we began to identify several areas of improvement to ChromBPNet—which we have now implemented in chrombpnet-refactor.

1. Support implementation across all 3 major deep learning libraries, on modern hardware: PyTorch, TensorFlow, JAX

Note: We are grateful for Eugenio Mattei at the Broad Institute for implementing an initial JAX version of the original ChromBPNet, that inspired our expansion to other libraries.

Problem: Deep learning advances rapidly. The development of the original ChromBPNet began in 2021, and was developed on top of the latest deep learning hardware and packages at the time: TensorFlow 2.8 on Python 3.8. However, the deep learning stack has evolved considerably since, making ChromBPNet often poorly compatible with current packages and hardware.

Solution: Here, we reimplemented ChromBPNet across all 3 major deep learning libraries: PyTorch 2.14.1 (default), TensorFlow 2.21.0 (with tf-keras 2.21.0, the Keras 2 API), and JAX 0.11.2 (with Keras 3.15.1), all behind one shared, framework-neutral pipeline. This allows better compatibility with modern hardware (e.g., H100/H200) and modern packages (e.g., tangermeme).

How to use: Each engine has its own conda environment. Create the ones you need from the repository root:

As default: We recommend using PyTorch, given broad compatibility with modern hardware and related deep learning packages (e.g., tangermeme).

2. Adaptable cut-site convention for DNase-seq

Note: We are grateful to our colleagues at Relation Therapeutics for identifying this issue to us.

Problem: ChromBPNet is a supervised deep learning model that takes as input a short length of DNA sequence (2,114 bp), and outputs the predicted chromatin accessibility of the given DNA sequence (at its center 1,000 bp) at single-base resolution for a set cell type. Hence, during model training, the model’s output is supervised by the experimental observed chromatin accessibility track for the input sequence at single-base resolution.

To construct the observed experimental signal track at single-base resolution, we first map the sequenced DNA fragments, and count only the 5’ end positions of each fragment. This prevents any read bias from fragments of different lengths. Next, we process the 5’ end reads, so that reads from the forward (+) stand and the reverse (-) strand line up with each other. This processing depends on the type of enzyme used, depending on the enzyme’s molecular mechanism of cleavage.

ATAC-seq: When Tn5 transposase–a homodimer enzyme–cleaves DNA, it creates two distal single-stranded cuts (using each subunit to create one cut each) that are 9 bp away from each other. Therefore, for the same cleavage event, if viewed as raw 5’ end reads, the read from the forward strand would be 9 bp away from the read from the reverse strand. Hence, depending on whether the cleavage event was identified by the forward strand or the reverse strand, the cleavage position would vary by 9 bp. Therefore, to synchronize the cuts from each strand to one coherent position, we symmetrically shift the forward strand forward by 4 bp (+4 shift), and shift the reverse strand backward by 4 bp (-4 shift). This allows the reads from either strand to pile up at the same position–at the center of the 9 bp gap. This has been the standard processing to construct the observed experimental track ATAC-seq in ChromBPNet, and remains consistent in chrombpnet-refactor.

(Note: The +4/-4 shift is a convention. E.g., Shifts of +5/-3, +3/-5, +7/-1, etc. would also all produce a feasible agreement between the forward and reverse strand reads. However, as the shift is asymmetric, the signal would be skewed towards one direction.)

DNase-seq: However, unlike Tn5 transposase, when DNase I–a monomer enzyme–cleaves DNA, it simply creates one double-stranded break at a single site. While mechanistically, this is easier to understand and visualize, when synchronizing reads from the forward strand and the reverse strand, as the gap is even (0 bp), there is no longer an integer shift that can align the reads symmetrically, and each read would need to be shifted by half a position (+0.5/-0.5) to stay symmetric–which in a discrete, one-hot coordinate system, is not possible.

Therefore, generally, we have the following options to shift and synchronize the fragments:

  • Option I: Align on the position of the forward strand’s read, by keeping the forward strand’s read position constant and shifting the reverse strand read to the right by 1 bp (0/+1; i.e., the “right-hand” convention)
  • Option II: Align on the position of the reverse strand’s read, by keeping the reverse strand’s read position constant and shifting the forward strand’s read to the left by 1 bp (-1/0; i.e., the “left-hand” convention)
  • Option III: Align on both the forward and reverse strand’s position, by assigning a read on both positions (-1/0 and 0/+1).

In the original ChromBPNet, we selected Option I: the right-hand side convention (0 bp shift on the positive strand, +1 bp shift on the negative strand). This ensured that irrespective of which direction of the break was sequenced, we stacked counts on the same position, despite the shift being asymmetric.

However, this asymmetric shift led to an issue during a step in training. During model training, since all chromatin is double-stranded but NGS sequencing typically only returns single-stranded reads, like many DNA sequence models, we could naturally augment and double the training data by simply taking the reverse complement of all the sequenced 5’ reads. And to avoid repeating a second batch of mapping and processing of raw reads, we could simply apply the reverse complement after the processing, directly on the aligned signal track. Previously, ChromBPNet also followed this training protocol: it was trained on a reverse complement-augmented training dataset, by doubling each raw data point by taking as input the forward and reverse complement DNA sequence one time each, and predicting the forward and reversed chromatin accessibility signal one time each.

When taking the reverse complement, the operation not only inverted the sequence and signal, but also inverted the shift convention. For a symmetric convention (e.g., in ATAC-seq), the inverted convention was still identical. However, for a asymmetric convention (e.g., in DNase-seq), the inversion led to two different conventions between the original data and the reverse complement-augmented data: right-hand convention for the original data, and left-hand convention for the reverse complement-augmented data. Effectively, this smeared the single-base pair resolution of the original training signal across to two adjacent base pairs.

This explained several phenomena we had observed.

Issue #1: ATAC-seq vs. DNase-seq: First, during evaluation of ChromBPNet, comparing model performance on ATAC-seq vs. DNase-seq, while the counts head prediction (= predicted total counts inside the 1 kb region) performance was comparable between the two assays (ATAC-seq: ~0.7 vs. DNase-seq: ~0.7; Pearson correlation; higher = better), the profile head prediction (= predicted distribution of the total counts, as a probability distribution; where counts x prediction = predicted signal) performance was significantly worse in DNase-seq (ATAC-seq: ~0.4–0.6 vs. DNase-seq: ~0.65–0.9; Jensen-Shannon distance (JSD); lower = better).

Issue #2: ChromBPNet vs. Other models (e.g., AlphaGenome): Second, this was also observed in benchmarks against other models. For example, in comparison with AlphaGenome, ChromBPNet’s profile prediction performance was only lower than AlphaGenome by 1.6% in ATAC-seq (by relative improvement in profile JSD), but was significantly lower in DNase-seq by 6.4%.

To investigate further, we performed two thorough evaluations on the impact of the shift convention bug, by training and comparing ChromBPNet models across six possible shift convention and data augmentation combinations, across 4 datasets (2 DNase-seq, 2 ATAC-seq):

Original ChromBPNet vs.: The original ChromBPNet’s right-hand side (0/+1) convention training data, with the reverse complement-augmented bug.

  1. forward-only: The original ChromBPNet’s right-hand side (0/+1) convention training data, but without the reverse complement-augmented data (i.e., half the training data).
  2. reverse complement-only: The original ChromBPNet’s “right-hand side (0/+1)” convention training using the reverse complement-augmented data only (i.e., the other half of the training data used to the train the original model; in other words, in reality, a left-hand side (-1/0) convention).
  3. right-hand: (Corrected) Right-hand side (0/+1) convention, in both forward and reverse complement-augmented data.
  4. left-hand: Left-hand side (-1/0) convention, in both forward and reverse complement-augmented data.
  5. both-side (half): Symmetric (-1/0 and 0/+1) convention, by assigning a half count to both sides.
  6. both-side (full): Symmetric (-1/0 and 0/+1) convention, by assigning full count to both sides (i.e., double the total counts).

Performance: We observed that using half the data (original, or augmented) alone already improved profile performance (in K562 DNase-seq, with performance defined as 1-JSD: original: 0.45 vs. forward-only/reverse complement-only: 0.50), while degrading counts head performance (original: 0.71 vs. forward-only/reverse complement-only: 0.68/0.69), since only half the data was used (the same drop was also observed in ATAC-seq: original 0.70 vs. forward-only/reverse complement-only: 0.68). The counts performance was fully recovered when a consistent convention was applied across the full, double-augmented data using either right-hand or left-hand side convention (original: 0.71 vs. right-hand/left-hand: 0.71), while the improved profile head performance was maintained (forward-only/reverse complement-only: 0.50 vs. right-hand/left-hand: 0.50). Both symmetric (-1/0 and 0/+1) conventions (half, full) showed recovered counts head performance (original: 0.71 vs. both-full/half: 0.70) and the highest profile head performance (forward-only/reverse complement-only/right-hand/left-hand: 0.50 vs. both-full/half: 0.56), due to smoothed profiles.

Cross-convention performance: Further, to understand how each model would perform based on the other’s shift convention, we performed cross-evaluation between the original and the newly trained models when tested on their corresponding test sets. For more detail, see Appendix: 2. Adaptable cut-site convention for DNase-seq (continued).

Overall, from the investigation, we could conclude that:

  • The model had (unintentionally) been trained on a mix of half right-hand side convention (from the original data) and half left-hand side convention (from the reverse complement-augmented data).
  • Hence, during prediction, the model was predicting an expectation between the two shift conventions.
  • This happened to be equivalent to the symmetric shift convention.

Solution: Moving forward, we have implemented the following fixes and suggestions:

  • DNase-seq cut site convention is now an optional argument (--dnase-convention): right (for right-hand side), left (for left-hand side), both-half (for symmetric, with half counts each; default), both-full (for both-symmetric, with full integer counts each), and right-deprecated (the original ChromBPNet’s behaviour, kept only to reproduce earlier runs). --data-aug separately chooses which examples are reverse complemented (augment, the default; forward-only; revcomp-only). This allows for all conventions to be possible during training as needed (user should remember which convention was used..!). The default setting is now both-half (for reasons explained next).
  • Previously trained ChromBPNet models predict a both-half-like convention. (And new, refactored default models will be backwards-compatible). We are currently in the process of updating the training, validation, and test set observed signals, as well as the correct convention-based model performance evaluations. We plan to share another update once this is ready.

3. Anthropic’s Claude Fable/Mythos 5.1 optimizations: Fused kernels and faster I/O (mostly faster I/O)

Midway through this refactoring, we head of an exciting new development: Anthropic’s recent model release of Fable/Mythos 5.1 and their case study of optimizing several DNA sequence models—including ChromBPNet. We always welcome any and all improvements to our models from the community, and thought that this refactoring opportunity would be a great chance for a deep dive into Fable/Mythos’ suggestions and incorporate them as needed. Anthropic’s optimizations were released on Sep 17, 2026.

Problem: Anthropic’s optimizations focused on drop-in optimization kits that speed up the inference pipeline of existing DNA sequence models. This includes preprocessing input data, loading model weights, model inference, any post-processing of the output data, and final write of the output file. Additionally, the optimizations ignore any partitions inside the model. For ChromBPNet, this meant optimizing the prediction pipeline pred_bw (which predicts over a set of regions and writes the predictions as BigWigs) using only the full model (which predicts with bias). The optimization kit reported a 1.6x speedup over the original implementation, on an NVIDIA H100.

Below is a summary of changes applied in the optimization kit from the model release of Fable/Mythos 5.1:

While the kit’s speedup is very useful for users of the original package (total: 4.4x on an L40S), interestingly, most of the kit’s gain came not from faster model inference (accounting for only 40% of the speed up), but by running CPU I/O in parallel with GPU inference (accounting for 60% of the speed up). In fact, writing the BigWig and metrics while the GPU predicts accounted for the largest gains (47% of the speed up), which, if allowing for flexible file size, could be further sped up by 3.1x with 1.5x smaller files using a fixed-step bigWig (implemented in Section 4; not part of Anthropic’s optimization kit). Further, while the kit’s Triton kernels did improve inference, the speed up was less pronounced compared to a standard PyTorch reimplementation: the forward pass takes 34.4 s (base) in the original TensorFlow 2.8, 15.1 s (2.3x) in PyTorch TF32, and 7.3 s (4.7x) with the kernels in TF32. In other words, TensorFlow 2.8’s forward itself was largely outdated and no longer optimized on modern hardware. Further, fused kernels cannot be used with DeepLIFT/DeepSHAP during interpretation without a specific rule for the new kernel (and hence also, cannot be used during training either). Therefore, we kept the Triton kernels as optional: --predict-fused-kernel-fast for TF32 precision (fast); --predict-fused-kernel-exact for fp32 precision (exact).

5. Reproducibility: Confirming parity with the original ChromBPNet

Given all the changes, we confirmed performance parity with the original ChromBPNet based on reproducibility on: (1) test set chromosome performance, and (2) model interpretation via DeepLIFT/DeepSHAP contribution scores.

Reproducibility of model performance is evidently important. Comparing the performance of the models trained on the refactored codebase (using the default both-half convention for DNase-seq), across all 3 deep learning engines (PyTorch, TensorFlow, JAX) against the (pretrained) original ChromBPNet model and an original ChromBPNet model retrained with the original codebase (the run-to-run reference): we observe near-perfect reproducibility across all 3 engines, in ATAC-seq and DNase-seq, in cell lines (K562) and primary cells (stomach), on counts/profile head performance, across all 5 chromosome folds. DNase-seq profile scores rise (K562: 0.33 to 0.48; stomach: 0.23 to 0.35, 1 - JSD) because the refactored models are trained and evaluated on the both-half signal: the original model scores as well on that test set (cross-evaluation above), so the gain reflects the evaluation target rather than a better model.

The robust model interpretation methods have been a key advantage of ChromBPNet, and hence reproducibility of model contribution scores was key. Comparing the DeepLIFT/DeepSHAP contribution scores from each model against the original ChromBPNet’s DeepLIFT/DeepSHAP scores, we observe that contribution scores can be difficult to reproduce perfectly, with only 20 reference sequences (optimized between reproducibility and compute). To quantify the variance in DeepLIFT/DeepSHAP contribution scores, we observed that DeepLIFT/DeepSHAP scores calculated using the same original, pretrained ChromBPNet model, but with simply a different random seed, could achieve a high but not perfect reproducibility (Pearson correlation: 0.86). Further, with a newly trained model, retrained using the same original ChromBPNet codebase, could only reach a reproducibility of 0.72 correlation, setting the expected upper bound for any reproducibility from a model trained on a changed codebase. Our refactored ChromBPNet codebase reaches this contribution score reproducibility: 0.71–0.72 across the 3 deep learning engines (PyTorch 0.711, TensorFlow 0.722, JAX 0.715). This confirmed the reproducibility of the refactored ChromBPNet codebase across test performance and contribution scores.

6. Supplementary: Additional improvements to model structure and usability (that deviate from original ChromBPNet)

Lastly, we’ve added a few non-backward compatible improvements to the model (that will remain optional, given the demand for backward compatibility).

Profile-only bias model

The original ChromBPNet pretrained a small bias model to learn the sequence preference of the assay enzyme (DNase-seq: DNase I, ATAC-seq: Tn5 transposase), predicting the enzyme’s effect on both the total accessibility across a 1,000 bp window (counts head prediction) and on the distribution of the sequencing reads (profile head prediction). However, the enzyme’s bias is typically not strong enough to affect the total accessibility summed across a 1,000bp window. Its effects are largely local, shifting the signal toward its preferred sequences. Hence, after training many ChromBPNet models for ENCODE GRAMMAR, we observed that the bias model was not affecting the counts head predictions, and almost exclusively contributing to the profile head predictions. Hence, in chrombpnet-refactor, we now train a bias model that predicts reads distribution only (profile head only), and is connected only to the profile head prediction of the full ChromBPNet model.

With the new profile-only bias model, we repeated the prediction and contribution score reproducibility analysis above, and observed similar reproducibility—confirming that using a profile-only bias model can reliably reproduce the original ChromBPNet’s performances.

Student-Teacher distillation

Another learning from ENCODE GRAMMAR (and following from the results shown by related models e.g., AlphaGenome): instead of using all k-fold models (from cross-validation) as an ensemble for downstream tasks (e.g., bias-corrected prediction, contribution scores), we have implemented an option to distill the ensemble of fold models (as a Teacher) into one (Student) model. During distillation, the single Student model trains on the k-fold ensemble’s mean predictions. The benefit of distilling k-fold folds into one model is the ease of use: instead of repeating k x downstream processes, then ensembling to obtain the average, downstream processes can now be performed on one Student model once—without losing much of the benefits of ensembling.

To preliminarily assess the reproducibility of distillation to match ensembling, we again repeated the prediction and contribution score reproducibility analysis performed above by comparing the 5-fold ensemble vs. a student model distilled on the 5 folds. We observed that the Student model could closely match the predictions and contribution scores of the 5-fold ensembling. Given that the ensembled models are used for most downstream processes, including more applied tasks such as variant effect prediction and TF motif analysis, we acknowledge that the new distilled models require the same scrutiny, and plan to evaluate its broad, applied performance in subsequent work. For best performance, we still recommend using the ensembled models, and only provide the distillation option as a convenience.

Refactored: chrombpnet-refactor

Overall, we share a reproducible, refactored version of ChromBPNet: supporting all 3 major deep learning engines, a corrected DNase-seq cut site convention, several internal and external optimizations—all with confirmed reproducibility in model performance and contributions. The scripts behind every figure and table in this post are in analysis/blog-2026-015/, with a README mapping each one to its commands.

Moving forward, we recommend using the refactored ChromBPNet codebase for all future use cases of ChromBPNet. And we continue to welcome any further improvements to the model and codebase.

Appendix: Adaptable cut-site convention for DNase-seq (continued)

Cross-convention performance: Along side models trained and tested on each shift convention and data augmention combination, we further performed a cross-evaluation between the newly trained models and their corresponding test set with the original model and its test set, by evaluating the newly trained models on the original model’s test set and vice versa.

We observed that the right-hand side convention model performed the best on the original ChromBPNet model’s test set on both matched counts head prediction (original: 0.71 vs. right-hand: 0.71) and improved profile head prediction (original: 0.44 vs. right-hand: 0.49), followed by forward-only model with similarly improved profile head prediction but degraded counts head prediction (as trained on half the data). Both symmetric conventions showed the same performance as the original model (original: 0.44 vs. both-half/full: 0.44), suggesting that the training scheme was effectively equivalent. And left-side convention and reverse complement-only performed the worst, with profile head prediction degrading dramatically as predictions were consistently off by one (due to convention differences), with only half the training data used by the reverse complement-only model. Lastly, evaluating the original ChromBPNet model on the newly processed test sets, the original model performed the best on the both symmetric convention test sets, as well as the actual symmetric models trained on its own data convention (original: 0.56 vs. both-half/full: 0.56), further confirming that the training schemes were effectively equivalent, and the original model was effectively predicting a symmetric convention signal. The original model showed equally degraded profile head performance on right-hand and left-hand side conventions, suggesting a symmetric prediction off by half the counts.

Overall, from the investigation, we could conclude that:

  • The model had (unintentionally) been trained on a mix of half right-hand side convention (from the original data) and half left-hand side convention (from the reverse complement-augmented data).
  • Hence, during prediction, the model was predicting an expectation between the two shift conventions.
  • This happened to be equivalent to the symmetric shift convention.
