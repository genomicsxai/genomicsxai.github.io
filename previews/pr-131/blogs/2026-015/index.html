Summary

ChromBPNet is a deep learning DNA sequence model that models chromatin accessibility, at base-resolution with DNase I/Tn5 transposase enzyme bias-factorization. Despite wide use of the model, there have been several improvements that we had been considering. Here, we apply those updates and present a refactored codebase of ChromBPNet, that systematically improves the following: (1) support for all 3 major deep learning frameworks, PyTorch, TensorFlow and JAX, on current releases and GPUs; (2) a corrected, selectable cut-site convention for DNase-seq; (3) the optimizations from Anthropic’s Claude Fable/Mythos 5.1 model release that apply to ChromBPNet’s pipeline; and (4) additional optimizations in pre- and post-processing–all with confirmed reproducibility to the original model. We share this study to highlight the benefits of early open access code to the community to help build better software and understanding together.

ChromBPNet: A bias factorized, base-resolution deep learning model of chromatin accessibility

ChromBPNet is a deep learning model of chromatin accessibility: how accessible a region of chromatin is–chromatin being the native DNA molecule in eukaryotic cells, that is in complex with other nuclear proteins and RNA to be packaged into a dense, compact structure–that in turn, determines how much the DNA is accessible to other cellular processes, including transcription. Experiments measure the level of accessibility of different regions of the genome in different cell types: “DNase-seq” uses the enzyme DNase I to cut exposed DNA; “ATAC-seq” uses the Tn5 transposase enzyme to insert sequencing adapters into accessible DNA. Depending on how accessible the region is, the enzymes DNase I/Tn5 transposase have more/less access to the DNA to perform their activities. By sequencing and mapping the resulting DNA fragments and their suggested enzyme activity sites, chromatin regions that contain many mapped fragments are quantified as highly accessible (as “peaks”) while regions with low fragments are quantified as inaccessible (as “non-peaks”).

However, the enzymes DNase I and Tn5 transposase also have preferences for particular DNA sequences, so the observed signal profiles reflect both genuine chromatin accessibility and assay-specific sequence bias. Separating these components is especially important when interpreting the signal at single-base resolution.

Here, ChromBPNet was developed to learn to predict chromatin accessibility signal from DNA sequence, at base-resolution with the explicit factorization of the enzyme’s sequence bias. ChromBPNet, together with robust model interpretation methods, has previously been shown to predict accessibility in unseen chromosomes, identify the binding preference and binding sites of known, cell type-specific transcription factors (TFs) that influence accessibility, and predict the effects of genomic variation on accessibility (caQTLs) across cell type and population–which we expanded to train 1,512 ChromBPNet models as part of ENCODE GRAMMAR.

However, while highly effective, like any model, ChromBPNet had room for improvements.

1. Support all 3 major deep learning libraries, on modern hardware: PyTorch, TensorFlow, JAX

Problem: Deep learning advances rapidly. While development of the original ChromBPNet began in 2021, and hence developed on the latest deep learning packages and hardware at the time–TensorFlow 2.8 on Python 3.8–the deep learning stack has rapidly accelerated since, making ChromBPNet incompatible with modern versions.

Solution: Here, we reimplemented ChromBPNet across all 3 major deep learning libraries: PyTorch 2.14 (default), TensorFlow 2.21 (with tf-keras, the Keras 2 API), and JAX 0.11 (with Keras 3), all behind one shared, framework-neutral pipeline.

How to use: Each engine has its own conda environment, because their frameworks cannot share one. Create the ones you need from the repository root:

EngineCreate the environmentThenPython and framework
PyTorch (default)conda env create -f envs/chrombpnet-pytorch.ymlconda activate chrombpnet-pytorchPython 3.12, PyTorch 2.14

Pick the engine with --engine (the checkpoint suffix decides when it is omitted) and the device with --device cpu, cuda or e.g. cuda:1. envs/README.md describes each engine, its GPU libraries, and what differs between them.

2. Adaptable cut-site convention for DNase-seq

Note: We are grateful to our colleagues at Relation Therapeutics for identifying this issue to us.

Problem: When constructing the experimental “ground truth" signal from the raw sequencing data, we map and count up the exact location that the enzyme was applied on the genome. For ATAC-seq, this is straightforward: Tn5 transposase acts as a homodimer to introduce a “tagmentation”–a single-stranded DNA break, followed by an immediate 5’-to-3’ ligation that attaches adapter sequences to the break (for later sequencing)–at two nearby sites that are 9 bp apart, one on each strand. Hence, to identify the exact position that the Tn5 transposase was applied, by convention, we take the middle of the two cut sites that are 9 bp apart as its position–which conveniently, coincides on top of a base (since the 9 bp gap is odd). When both ends of a region of chromatin is “tagmented”, the region is no longer attached to the original long chromatin and free as a DNA fragment, which can then be sequenced by next generation sequencing (NGS). Hence, to identify the position of the Tn5 transposase from only one DNA fragment, we trace back the other cut site that must have occurred 9 bp away, and identify its middle position, which is equivalent to positions +4 bp or -4 bp (depending on the strand direction) from the 5’ end of the sequenced fragment (only a read’s 5’ end marks a cut; its 3’ end is merely where sequencing stopped. With paired-end data, the 5’ ends of the two mates are the fragment’s two ends, so both cuts are counted). This results in one distinct base pair position per enzyme effect site.

However, for DNase-seq, identifying the exact position of the enzyme activity is trickier: DNase I cleaves at just one site and the “middle” position is not on top of a specific base pair position, but between two base pairs, i.e., on a specific bond position. For DNase-seq, DNase I only introduces one cut: a single double-stranded break between two base pairs. Hence, the “position” of the DNase I would ideally also be at the middle of the two base pairs, or on the cleaved bond itself. However, since we in genomics use one-hot-style sequence encoding, we must place the DNase I on some base pair position. By convention, we can either place the DNase I on the right-hand side of the break (which would imply the positions 0 bp or +1 bp from the sequenced 5’ end, depending on the strand), or on the left-hand side of the break (which would imply positions -1 bp or 0 bp from the sequenced 5’ end, depending on the strand), or on both sides of the break (which would imply positions 0 and -1, or 0 and +1, depending on the strand). In the original ChromBPNet, we had selected the right-hand side convention: 0 bp on the positive strand, +1 bp on the negative strand. This ensured that irrespective of which direction of the break was sequenced, we stacked counts on the same base pair position.

However, the issue arises during reverse complement augmentation of the data. In DNA sequence models, we are often (un)fortunate to double our data for free/need to take into account the opposite strand’s sequence, by taking the reverse complement of the original data. ChromBPNet also followed this training protocol: for a random half of the training examples, it takes the reverse complement of the input sequence and reverses the signal profile. However, when taking the reverse complement of the sequence, we cannot simply also take the reverse complement of the signal: the reverse complement not only inverts the sequence, but also inverts the enzyme position convention. For a single symmetric convention, such as ATAC-seq, the inversion is still identical. However, for a non-symmetric convention, such as DNase-seq, the inversion led to two different conventions between the original data and the reverse complement-augmented data: right-hand convention for the original data, and left-hand convention for the reverse complement-augmented data.

This explained several phenomena we had observed. First, during evaluation of ChromBPNet, comparing model performance on ATAC-seq vs. DNase-seq, while the counts head prediction (= predicted total counts inside the 1 kb region) performance was comparable between the two assays (ATAC-seq: ~0.7 vs. DNase-seq: ~0.7; Pearson correlation; higher = better), the profile head prediction (= predicted distribution of the total counts, as a probability distribution; where counts x prediction = predicted signal) performance was significantly worse in DNase-seq (ATAC-seq: ~0.4–0.6 vs. DNase-seq: ~0.65–0.9; Jensen-Shannon distance (JSD); lower = better).

Second, this was also observed in benchmarks against other models: in comparison with AlphaGenome, which improved on ChromBPNet’s 1 bp profile predictions by only 1.6% in ATAC-seq but by 6.4% in DNase-seq (relative improvement in profile JSD).

Third, we could artificially reproduce this performance gap: As the same original ChromBPNet model trained on K562 DNase-seq (using the unintentional convention-inversion during reverse complement-augmentation) as the baseline, we compared the model’s performance against those retrained on 6 different training data sources: (1) forward-only: the original ChromBPNet’s right-hand side (0/+1) convention training data, but without the reverse complement-augmented data (i.e., half the training data), (2) reverse complement-only: the original ChromBPNet’s “right-hand side (0/+1) ” convention training using the reverse complement-augmented data only (i.e., the other half the training data; hence, in reality left-hand side (-1/0) convention), (3) full right-hand side (0/+1) convention, (4) left-hand side (-1/0) convention, (5) both-side (0 and -1/0 and +1) convention by assigning half count to both sides, and (6) both-side (0 and -1/0 and +1) convention by assigning full integer count to both sides (i.e., double the total counts). We observed that using half the data (original, or augmented) improved profile performance (in K562 DNase-seq: original: 0.45 vs. forward-only/reverse complement-only: 0.50), while degrading counts head performance (original: 0.71 vs. forward-only/reverse complement-only: 0.68/0.69), since only half the data was used (the same drop was also observed in ATAC-seq: original 0.70 vs. forward-only/reverse complement-only: 0.68). The counts performance was fully recovered when the correct convention was applied across the full, double-augmented data using either right-hand or left-hand side convention (original: 0.71 vs. right-hand/left-hand: 0.71), while the improved profile head performance was maintained (forward-only/reverse complement-only: 0.50 vs. right-hand/left-hand: 0.50). Both both-side (0 and -1/0 and +1) conventions (half, full) showed recovered counts head performance (original: 0.71 vs. both-full/half: 0.70) and the highest profile head performance (forward-only/reverse complement-only/right-hand/left-hand: 0.50 vs. both-full/half: 0.56), due to smoothed profiles.

Further, we also investigated the cross-evaluation between the newly trained models and their corresponding test set with the original model and its test set, by evaluating the newly trained models on the original model’s test set and vice versa. We observed that the right-hand side convention model performed the best on the original ChromBPNet model’s test set on both matched counts head prediction (original: 0.71 vs. right-hand: 0.71) and improved profile head prediction (original: 0.44 vs. right-hand: 0.49), followed by forward-only model with similarly improved profile head prediction but degraded counts head prediction (as trained on half the data). Both both-side conventions showed the same performance as the original model (original: 0.44 vs. both-half/full: 0.44), suggesting that the training scheme was effectively equivalent. And left-side convention and reverse complement-only performed the worst, with profile head prediction degrading dramatically as predictions were constantly off by one (due to convention differences), with only half the training data used by the reverse complement-only model. Lastly, evaluating the original ChromBPNet model on the newly processed test sets, the original model performed the best on the both both-side convention test sets, as well as the actual both-side models trained on its own data convention (original: 0.56 vs. both-half/full: 0.56), further confirming that the training schemes were effectively equivalent, and the original model was effectively predicting a both-side convention signal. The original model showed equally degraded profile head performance on right-hand and left-hand side conventions, suggesting a symmetric prediction off by half the counts.

From the investigation, we could conclude what was happening: the model, (unintentionally) trained on half right-hand side convention (from the original data) and half left-hand side convention (from the reverse complement-augmented data), was predicting an expectation between the two data conventions, which happened to coincide with the both-side convention.

Solution: Moving forward, we have implemented the following fixes and suggestions:

  • DNase-seq cut site convention is now an optional argument (--dnase-convention): right (for right-hand side), left (for left-hand side), both-half (for both-side, with half counts each; default), both-full (for both-side, with full integer counts each), and right-deprecated (the original ChromBPNet’s behaviour, kept only to reproduce earlier runs). --data-aug separately chooses which examples are reverse complemented (augment, the default; forward-only; revcomp-only). This allows for all conventions to be possible during training as needed (user should remember which convention was used..!). The default setting is now both-half (for reasons explained next).
  • Previously trained ChromBPNet models predict a both-half-like convention. (And new, refactored default models will be backwards-compatible). We are currently in the process of updating the training, validation, and test set observed signals, as well as the correct convention-based model performance evaluations. We plan to share another update once this is ready.

3. Anthropic’s Claude Fable/Mythos 5.1 optimizations: Fused kernels and faster I/O (mostly faster I/O)

An exciting side-project to this refactoring was hearing about Anthropic’s recent model release of Fable/Mythos 5.1 and their case study of optimizing several DNA sequence models–including ChromBPNet. We always welcome any and all improvements to our models from the community, and thought that this refactoring opportunity would be a great chance to deep dive into Fable/Mythos’ suggestions and incorporate them as needed. Anthropic’s optimizations were released on Sep 17, 2026.

Problem: Anthropic’s optimizations focused on drop-in optimization kits that speed up the inference pipeline of existing DNA sequence models. This includes preprocessing input data, loading model weights, model inference, any post-processing output data, and final write of the output file. For ChromBPNet, this meant optimizing the prediction pipeline pred_bw, which predicts over a set of regions and writes the predictions as BigWigs. The optimization kit reported a 1.6x speedup over the original implementation, on an NVIDIA H100.

Below is a summary of changes applied in the optimization kit from the model release of Fable/Mythos 5.1:

While a 1.6x speed up is generally very useful, especially for users, interestingly, in our port most of the kit’s gain came not from faster model inference but by running CPU I/O in parallel with GPU inference: writing each model’s BigWig and metrics in a worker process while the next model predicts (~8% of wall clock), and importing the post-processing libraries in the background (~11%). If allowing for flexible file size and constraining only for identical value, the write of the BigWig could be further sped up by 2.8x with 1.5x smaller files using fixed-step bigWigs (implemented in Section 4; not part of Anthropic’s optimization kit). Further, the kit’s fused kernels did not actually show speed up–in fact, it slowed down by 11%, even on larger GPUs (e.g., H200), with only some very minor gains in numerical accuracy, using fp32-exact kernels (vs. eager TF32 convolutions). Given the limited use of the kernels, we kept them as optional (can be used with --predict-fused-kernel).

Reproducibility: Confirming parity with the original ChromBPNet

Given all the changes, we confirmed performance parity with the original ChromBPNet based on reproducibility on: (1) test set chromosome performance, and (2) model interpretation via DeepLIFT/DeepSHAP contribution scores.

Reproducibility of model performance is evidently important. Comparing the performance of the models trained on the refactored codebase (using the default both-half convention for DNase-seq), across all 3 deep learning engines (PyTorch, TensorFlow, JAX) against the (pretrained) original ChromBPNet model and an original ChromBPNet model retrained with the original codebase (the run-to-run reference): we observe near-perfect reproducibility across all 3 engines, in ATAC-seq and DNase-seq, in cell lines (K562) and primary cells (stomach), on counts/profile head performance, across all 5 chromosome folds. DNase-seq profile scores rise (K562: 0.44 to 0.56; stomach: 0.36 to 0.46, 1 - JSD) because the refactored models are trained and evaluated on the both-half signal: the original model scores the same 0.56 on that test set (cross-evaluation above), so the gain reflects the evaluation target rather than a better model.

The robust model interpretation methods have been a key advantage of ChromBPNet, and hence reproducibility of model contribution scores was key. Comparing the DeepLIFT/DeepSHAP contribution scores from each model against the original ChromBPNet’s DeepLIFT/DeepSHAP scores, we observe that contribution scores can be difficult to reproduce perfectly, with only 20 reference sequences (optimized between reproducibility and compute). To quantify the variance in DeepLIFT/DeepSHAP contribution scores, we observed that DeepLIFT/DeepSHAP scores calculated using the same original, pretrained ChromBPNet model, but with simply a different random seed, could achieve a high but not perfect reproducibility (Pearson correlation: 0.86). Further, with a newly trained model, retrained using the same original ChromBPNet codebase, could only reach a reproducibility of 0.72 correlation, setting the expected upper bound for any reproducibility from a model trained on a changed codebase. Our refactored ChromBPNet codebase reaches this contribution score reproducibility: 0.71–0.72 across the 3 deep learning engines (PyTorch 0.711, TensorFlow 0.722, JAX 0.715). This confirmed the reproducibility of the refactored ChromBPNet codebase across test performance and contribution scores.

Overall, we share a fully reproducible, refactored version of ChromBPNet: supporting all 3 major deep learning engines, a corrected DNase-seq cut site convention, several internal and external optimizations–all with confirmed reproducibility in model performance and contributions.

Moving forward, we recommend using the refactored ChromBPNet codebase for all future use cases of ChromBPNet. And we continue to invite any further improvements to the model and codebase!
